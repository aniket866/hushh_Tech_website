## What Makes a Business "Great"?

#### Chapter 1: The Philosophy

A great business is not simply a profitable one. It is one where the competitive advantage is structural, durable, and self-reinforcing. Where every dollar reinvested earns more than the cost of that capital. Where the management team treats shareholders as partners, not passengers. And where the financial posture is so strong that adversity becomes opportunity rather than existential threat.

The Berkshire framework makes this explicit: invest in businesses that are thoroughly understood, with durable advantages and long-term prospects, led by high-integrity, owner-minded leaders, while maintaining financial strength as a strategic asset. This is not a checklist. It is a philosophy that filters out 95% of the investable universe before price is even considered.

## The Five Business Quality Filters

#### Investment Criteria

#### Durable Moat

Persistently high gross and operating margins, high ROIC over full business cycles, and predictable free cash flow that survives recessions, rate cycles, and competitive attacks.

#### High & Resilient ROIC

Returns on invested capital that stay elevated over time — not just in peak years. The durability of ROIC, not its peak, is what separates compounders from cyclicals.

#### Conservative Balance Sheet

A "fortress-like" financial posture: low leverage, strong coverage ratios, and liquidity that turns downturns into buying opportunities rather than survival tests.

#### Shareholder-Aligned Management

Owner-minded leaders who allocate capital rationally — buybacks only below intrinsic value, reinvestment only where ROIC exceeds cost of capital, and dividends that grow with earnings.

#### Valuation Discipline

A margin of safety relative to conservatively determined intrinsic value. No matter how great the business, price paid determines the return earned. The expected compounding of intrinsic value per share must be attractive relative to price.

## Owner Earnings: The Only Number That Truly Matters

#### Valuation Framework

Warren Buffett's "owner earnings" concept — reported earnings plus non-cash charges minus the ongoing capital required to maintain competitive position — is the economic foundation of intrinsic value. It is elegant in concept and demanding in practice, because maintenance capex is inherently an estimate. Great analysts embrace this ambiguity rather than hiding behind accounting precision.

#### Method 1: FCF Yield Normalization

Free cash flow as a conservative owner-earnings proxy when capex is not structurally distorted. "Fair value" is derived by assuming a target FCF yield consistent with the business's durability and growth profile. Margin of safety is the gap between current FCF yield and that target.

#### Method 2: Relative Multiples

EV/EBITDA or P/E compared to a conservative "quality multiple" range for the moat strength and cyclicality profile. Used when current FCF is temporarily depressed by reinvestment cycles — notably for megacap platform builders and infrastructure providers in heavy capex phases.

#### Method 3: DCF-Lite (Boundary Check)

Used sparingly — as a sanity check rather than a full model. Applied to establish a range of intrinsic values when FCF normalization alone is insufficient given data extraction limits. Never used as a point estimate.
